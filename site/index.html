The biggest crypto theft on record

It is more than four times the Bybit hack of February 2025, which until now was the biggest theft from an exchange. The FBI blamed North Korea for that one.

Dollar value at the time of each theft. LuBian, a Chinese bitcoin mining pool, never confirmed its loss: the analytics firm Arkham found it in 2025, almost five years after the coins left. Poly Network got most of its money back. Other figures as listed by Comparitech and, for Bitget, Bankless.

How they did it

An exchange keeps most of its customers' money offline. It keeps a smaller amount in online "hot wallets" so it can pay withdrawals quickly, and its software signs each payment out of them automatically.

Binance said early on 30 October that the attacker got in through one of its engineers' laptops, reached the system that sends withdrawals, and used it to send their own (reported by FinanceFeeds). It says its private keys were not stolen. The blockchains fit that: every payment to the attacker was signed by Binance's own wallets, in the usual way.

On Ethereum the attacker went further. They got Binance's system to sign 50 copies of a permission that handed control of one wallet to a program they had written. One copy matched the wallet's next transaction number, and three minutes later the program emptied the wallet in a single transaction: 167,464 Ether and 63 other tokens, for a fee of less than a dollar.

The payments were not built like Binance's normal ones. In the Ethereum block where a second wallet was emptied, Binance's own payments paid the fee its software always pays, and the nine payments to the attacker paid more than twice that. Every amount taken was rounded to one decimal place, such as 7,636.8 bitcoin. That looks like the attacker's software making the requests and Binance's system signing them.

They had been preparing for at least a day and a half. The first money for the attack came out of Tornado Cash, a service that hides where coins came from, on the morning of 28 October. The next afternoon they rehearsed the Ethereum trick on wallets of their own.

TRM Labs, a blockchain analysis firm, says North Korea's TraderTraitor group did it and that it is highly confident. The blockchains alone can't show who it was.

Binance wallet 14 was an ordinary wallet: anything signed with its key, it obeys. At 20:20:59 the attacker sent one transaction carrying 50 signatures made with that key, each one telling the wallet to run the attacker's program from then on.

Counter already used by Binance: 34The one that landed: 18,914,809Never reached: 15

A signature like this works for one value of the wallet's transaction counter. Binance's own system was sending from wallet 14 all evening, so the counter kept moving. The attacker signed for the next 50 values and sent them all at once; whichever matched when the block was built would take effect. The 35th did.

How they got the signatures

Binance said at 02:25 UTC on 30 October that its first findings point to a DevOps engineer's laptop: through it the attacker reached a service container and sent withdrawals, and Binance has found no sign that its private keys leaked (reported by FinanceFeeds). That fits what the chain shows: Binance kept using the same wallets all night, so its own signing system was made to sign, on nine blockchains within half an hour. Binance put the total at about $6.3 billion, the same as this page. TRM Labs attributes it with high confidence to TraderTraitor, North Korea's state hacking unit, on intelligence from on and off the chain; the chain alone does not show who did it. TRM counts ten blockchains and $6.36 billion; this page reads nine.

The withdrawals were not built like Binance's own. In the Ethereum block where wallet 16 was emptied, Binance's own two payments from it paid the usual 1 gwei tip with a fixed gas limit; the nine to the attacker paid 2.4 gwei with exact limits. Tron, the XRP Ledger and Bitcoin show the same kind of difference, and every amount taken was cut to one decimal place, such as 7,636.8 BTC. That points to the attacker's software making the requests and Binance's system signing them.

Method

On Ethereum the starting points are the five attack transactions above, decoded with eth_getTransactionByHash. From there every transfer of ETH or a priced ERC-20 worth $10K or more is recorded. A plain wallet (no code) or Safe multisig that receives $1M or more from the attacker's side is followed in turn, up to four hops.

Contracts are not followed. Named ones are grouped by what they do. Unnamed ones are grouped together: they may be trading routers or the attacker's own contracts, and this page can't tell which. Neither group's balances count as the attacker's. A wallet with an EIP-7702 delegation has code but is still a wallet, and is followed. A few roles were set by hand after checking on-chain: two Safes that took back Synthetix and Sandbox tokens, and two hop wallets the first pass took for outside senders.

Totals: every dollar that leaves Binance for the attacker's side lands in "still movable" and from then on is in exactly one place, so the five columns always add up to what was taken. Money sent to an exchange or lender goes to "in swaps and loans" and comes back out when the service pays (a swap's Ether, an Aave loan), so that column is net. A freeze moves the frozen wallet's balance of that token to "frozen". Money sent on in amounts under $1M goes to wallets this page does not follow: "not traced". Each dot is $1M. Ether is valued at today's price, dollar tokens at $1, other tokens at the price when the transfer was recorded.

Wallet map: each dot stands for $25,000 ($60,000 on phones) and takes the exact path its money took, transfer by transfer. Dollar tokens turn blue once their issuer has blocked the wallet they sit in.

Ether moved by a contract inside a transaction (an internal transaction, such as the 167,464 ETH in the main sweep, or a router paying out a swap) is read from Blockscout's internal-transactions list, which its transactions list leaves out.

The other chains: Bitcoin, Tron, XRP Ledger, Solana, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base and Avalanche are read the same way and add into the same totals: the Binance wallets that paid the attacker that night, every movement of $10K or more from there, and plain wallets that got $1M or more followed up to four hops. Each chain's own coin is valued at today's price. Tether's freezes on Tron and Avalanche count as frozen. On Arbitrum the attacker's funds were moved to a locked address at 00:18 on 30 Oct by a transaction sent from Ethereum; that counts as frozen too. BNB Chain was checked and showed nothing. The Binance wallets on the XRP Ledger carry Binance's verified name; on Bitcoin an outside label and its history; on the other chains their history only, because their explorers block automated reads.

Not covered: USD1 and PAXG freezes reported by World Liberty and Paxos. Ripple reported freezing about $200M of RLUSD; 40.5M is frozen on Ethereum, and that is what this page counts.

Data comes from the Blockscout API and the PublicNode Ethereum RPC; for the other chains from mempool.space, TronGrid, the XRP Ledger's public servers, Solana's public RPC, each layer-2's public RPC and Routescan, with prices from Coinbase, Kraken and CoinGecko. A job that runs about every 15 minutes and publishes the result with this page; your browser reads only this site.
